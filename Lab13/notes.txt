Lab 13 — Semaphores in Your Own Kernel
Tacu Igor — FAF-242

1. Why do letters mix without the lock? How many calls per line?
Each process prints 30 letters plus a newline using write(1, p, 1): 31
system calls per line. Scheduling can switch processes between these
individual writes, and three QEMU CPUs can execute them concurrently.
Console synchronization protects each write's internal work, not the
whole user-space line, so the two sequences may interleave. A particular
unlocked line can also happen to remain whole.

2. What makes a whole line a critical section?
In user/mix.c, sem_down(3) before the character loop and sem_up(3) after
it delimit the critical section. Semaphore 3 starts at 1. The permit
covers all 31 writes, including the newline. Another cooperating process
cannot enter its line loop until the permit is returned. This guarantees
whole lines, not which process prints first or FIFO fairness.

3. Why while, not if, in sem_down?
sem_up adds one permit and wakeup(s) wakes all processes registered on s.
After one awakened process acquires the spinlock and takes the permit,
another awakened process can reacquire the lock and find value zero.
It must recheck the condition and sleep again. Being awakened is a reason
to retry, not a reservation of a permit. sleep_prepare(s) registers the
channel while the semaphore lock is still held; this tree's wakeup clears
the registered channel even before sleep() runs, preventing a semaphore
post in that interval from being lost.

4. Why release the spinlock before sleep?
A waiting process must let sem_up acquire the semaphore lock to add a
permit and wake it. Sleeping while holding that lock would prevent the
operation needed to release the waiter; it also violates xv6 scheduler
rules for sleeping with unrelated spinlocks held. The protocol registers
the wait channel first, releases the semaphore lock, sleeps, then acquires
the semaphore lock again and rechecks the value before decrementing it.
